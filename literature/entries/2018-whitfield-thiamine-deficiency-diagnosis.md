# Summary

- Comprehensive review of thiamine deficiency disorders with emphasis on diagnostic biomarkers, prevalence estimation challenges, and public-health control.
- Highlights that clinical manifestations are highly variable and that lack of a universally agreed, rapidly available biomarker complicates diagnosis and epidemiology.
- Compares direct measures (thiamine and thiamine diphosphate in whole blood/erythrocytes) with functional assays (erythrocyte transketolase activity / ETKAC).
- Notes plasma/serum free thiamine largely reflects recent intake and is a smaller blood pool than erythrocyte TDP.

# Key data points

- Whole-blood or erythrocyte thiamine diphosphate (ThDP/TDP) is preferred for status assessment in many modern labs.
- Majority (~80%) of whole-blood thiamine content is present in erythrocytes as ThDP; plasma/serum free thiamine is a smaller pool that largely reflects recent intake.
- Plasma/serum thiamine has poor sensitivity/specificity for tissue stores and is a **bidirectional** weak status indicator (neither high nor low plasma values alone reliably prove or exclude tissue deficiency).
- ETK is considered more informative functionally (demonstrates actual enzyme functionality). ETKAC is currently the best biochemical evidence of deficiency but is limited by availability (few laboratories).
- Clinical diagnosis remains critical because turnaround and assay availability vary widely.
- Reference ranges and cutoffs differ by method, lab, and population.

- Assay recommendations have continued to evolve since 2018 (LC-MS/MS whole-blood TDP increasingly preferred).
