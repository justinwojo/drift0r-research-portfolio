# Summary

- Acquired copper deficiency causes a **myeloneuropathy** resembling B12-deficiency subacute combined degeneration: sensory ataxia, spastic gait, dorsal-column signs, often with axonal peripheral neuropathy.
- Hematologic clues include anemia ± neutropenia (not always present).
- Common drivers: prior gastric surgery, malabsorption, zinc excess (including some denture creams), parenteral nutrition errors.
- Copper repletion can stabilize or partially reverse deficits; delayed treatment risks permanent cord injury.

# Key data points

- MRI may show dorsal-column T2 hyperintensity; may be normal early.
- Check serum copper, ceruloplasmin, and zinc together; 24h urine copper sometimes used.
- Neurologic disease can occur **without** frank hematologic abnormalities.
- Distinct from Wilson disease (copper overload), which requires different testing (see lit-0198).

**Why:**
- **Matches (partial):** History of severe neuropathy (though labeled and treatment-responsive as **thiamine**/dry beriberi), multi-level spine disease and sensory symptoms, nutrient-deficiency theme, young adult with complex neurology.
- **Does not match as primary unifier:** Documented B1 deficiency with rapid benfotiamine benefit points first to thiamine pathways. Copper deficiency does not produce CRP-normal migratory polyarthralgia, severe primary-looking osteoporosis as the headline, hypogonadism, HαT, or IgG subclass skew. No gastric bypass or zinc excess history noted in the public pack.
- **Why still catalogued:** Copper (and zinc) labs are cheap “don’t miss” tests in unexplained myeloneuropathy and can coexist with other deficiencies.

# Caveats

- Do not confuse with Wilson disease (opposite copper problem).
- Over-replacement or misdiagnosis without levels is unhelpful.
- Hematologic normalcy does not exclude neurologic copper deficiency.
