# Summary

- Endocrine Society CPG on diagnosing and treating male hypogonadism and testosterone (T) therapy.
- Diagnosis requires symptoms/signs of T deficiency **plus** unequivocally and consistently low morning fasting total T (confirm with repeat); free T if SHBG-altering conditions or borderline total T.
- **Primary vs secondary:** measure LH and FSH — high gonadotropins = primary (testicular); low/inappropriately normal = secondary (pituitary–hypothalamic).
- Secondary hypogonadism warrants further workup (prolactin, iron studies, other pituitary hormones, MRI when indicated) because treatable central causes exist.
- TRT is appropriate for organic hypogonadism with low T; not recommended for age-related decline alone without confirmed deficiency and symptoms.
- Exogenous T suppresses spermatogenesis; men desiring near-term fertility should not start TRT (prefer gonadotropins/SERMs in selected secondary cases).

# Key data points

- Diagnostic threshold context: many labs use total T ~264–300 ng/dL lower limit; patient historical T ~34 ng/dL is far below diagnostic cutoffs (unequivocal deficiency if confirmed).
- LH/FSH distinguish primary vs secondary (strong recommendation).
- Monitor hematocrit, PSA (age-appropriate), symptoms, and on-treatment T.
- Offer T only after shared decision-making on benefits/risks (erythrocytosis, prostate surveillance, fertility).

# Caveats

- Not medical advice; guideline for clinicians, not a diagnosis for this case.
- Off-label SERM/clomiphene use is discussed more in urology literature than fully endorsed as first-line TRT alternative here.
- Age/comorbidity assumptions differ from a ~30s idiopathic presentation with markedly low BMD.
