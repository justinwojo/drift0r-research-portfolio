# Summary

- Broad clinical review of kidney-stone pathogenesis, evaluation, and management with attention to metabolic risk factors.
- Highlights that both highly acidic urine (≤~5.5) and highly alkaline urine (≥~6.7) predispose to calcium stone disease, with alkaline urine favoring calcium phosphate phases.
- Covers hypercalciuria, hypocitraturia, volume, and dietary factors as modifiable supersaturation drivers.
- Useful framework for interpreting a hypercalciuric patient with mid-alkaline urine pH who is not a classic acidic-urine uric acid stone former.

# Key data points

- Disturbances in urinary pH: acidic urine promotes uric acid and favors some CaOx environments; alkaline urine raises CaP supersaturation.
- Calcium phosphate stones associate with higher urine pH and metabolic conditions (RTA, primary hyperparathyroidism, etc.) as well as idiopathic CaP formers.
- Hypercalciuria remains a central driver of calcium stone risk independent of composition.
- Evaluation pillars: stone analysis when available, 24h urine chemistry (volume, Ca, oxalate, citrate, uric acid, Na, pH, creatinine), serum Ca/PTH/electrolytes.
- Prevention principles: fluid, diet (Na, animal protein, adequate calcium), and targeted drugs (thiazides, alkali, allopurinol) matched to chemistry—not one-size-fits-all.

# Caveats

- pH 6.53 is “alkaline-leaning” but not extreme; single values fluctuate with diet, citrate, and infection.
- One lifetime stone under dehydration limits generalizability of recurrent-stone algorithms.
- Alkali therapy for bone/citrate can further raise pH—tradeoff if CaP is a concern.
