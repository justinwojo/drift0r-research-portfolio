# Summary

- NEJM Clinical Practice review of osteoporosis diagnosis and management specifically in men.
- Stresses that osteoporosis in men is often secondary; history, exam, and routine labs identify most causes.
- Common secondary drivers: hypogonadism, glucocorticoids, alcohol, smoking, low calcium intake, vitamin D deficiency.
- Baseline labs typically include CBC, LFTs, TSH, testosterone, creatinine, calcium, 25-OH vitamin D.
- DXA interpretation and treatment thresholds discussed for men; pharmacologic options include bisphosphonates and anabolic therapy.
- Testosterone therapy considered for hypogonadal men with osteoporosis, with caveats on fracture-endpoint data.
- Emphasizes under-recognition of male osteoporosis in clinical practice.
- Still frequently cited as a practical bedside reference.

# Key data points

- Secondary causes present in a large fraction of men with osteoporosis (often cited 50–70% in related literature).
- Recommended initial laboratory panel for all men with osteoporosis.
- Pharmacotherapy indicated for men with fractures or osteoporosis-range BMD after secondary evaluation.
- Lifestyle: calcium, vitamin D, weight-bearing exercise, limit alcohol/tobacco.

# Similarity to Drift0r case

- Historical severe hypogonadism maps directly to Ebeling’s secondary-cause hierarchy.
- Supports comprehensive labs even when Ca/PTH/VitD are currently normal.
- Young male with extreme T-scores fits “suspect secondary disease first.”
- Frames testosterone history as etiologically relevant even if not the sole ongoing driver.

# Caveats

- 2008; drug options and some densitometry standards have evolved.
- No abstract on PubMed; full text paywalled.
- Limited discussion of idiopathic osteoporosis bone-formation defects and high-turnover phenotypes.
