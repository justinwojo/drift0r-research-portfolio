# Summary

- *Coxiella burnetii* causes Q fever; acute disease is often pneumonia/hepatitis/fever, while **chronic Q fever** targets heart valves, vascular prostheses, and sometimes bone.
- Osteoarticular chronic infection: osteomyelitis, spondylodiscitis, tenosynovitis—can be culture-negative and indolent.
- Diagnosis uses serology (phase I IgG titers for chronic disease), PCR on blood/tissue, and imaging of valves/vessels/bone.
- Risk groups: older adults, immunosuppression, valvulopathy, aneurysms/grafts; exposure via aerosols from infected ruminants/parturient cats, unpasteurized dairy.

# Key data points

- Chronic Q fever is defined more by persistent focus + serology than by symptom duration alone (nomenclature evolved).
- Inflammatory markers often elevated with endovascular infection; focal osteomyelitis may be subtler.
- Treatment of chronic foci is prolonged combination antibiotics (specialist ID)—not summarized as advice here.
- Geographic exposure history (farms, abattoirs, windborne outbreaks) raises pretest probability.

**Score:** 1–2

**Why:**
- **Matches (partial):** Chronic multi-system pain; night sweats in narrative; transient improvement on certain antibiotics (nonspecific); culture-negative osteoarticular infection is a known mimic; Texas rural/suburban exposure possible but not documented.
- **Against:** Years of **normal CRP/ESR** argue against active endovascular chronic Q fever. No prosthetic valve/graft. markedly low BMD for age + B1 deficiency + HαT + hypogonadism not explained by Coxiella. Imaging has not shown classic Q-fever osteomyelitis foci in the public summaries.
- **Testing note:** Phase I/II serology is reasonable if ID workup expands—especially with new fever, endocarditis signs, or osteomyelitis imaging.

# Caveats

- Serology interpretation is specialist-level (cutoff titers, cross-reactivity).
- Antibiotic responses in complex patients are non-diagnostic.
