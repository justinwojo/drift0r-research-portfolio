# Summary

- Reviews why thiamine deficiency is common in sepsis/septic shock and how high catabolic/infectious stress rapidly depletes limited B1 stores.
- Connects thiamine’s role in aerobic metabolism (PDH) to lactate elevation and shock physiology that can mimic or worsen critical illness.
- Summarizes epidemiology (deficiency estimates in sepsis ranging widely, often substantial) and clinical trial signals for targeted thiamine therapy.
- Directly supports the concept of **infection/inflammation-driven thiamine consumption**.

# Key data points

- Body thiamine stores are small; deficiency can develop over weeks with poor intake—faster with high metabolic demand.
- Sepsis prevalence estimates of thiamine deficiency roughly 10–70% depending on population and assay.
- Shared features of B1 deficiency and septic shock: high lactate, acidosis, hypotension—diagnostic overlap.
- Supplementation is low-risk; evidence for outcome benefit is promising but heterogeneous across trials.
- Mechanisms: increased utilization, impaired intake/absorption, increased losses (e.g., diuretics, filtration) in critical care.

# Caveats

- Evidence base is ICU sepsis, not outpatient occult infection or tick-borne syndromes.
- Does not prove that suspected Bartonella/Babesia (or other) infection caused his beriberi.
- Dietary intake, absorption, and genetic metabolic factors also matter.
- Not medical advice; thiamine decisions belong with treating clinicians.
