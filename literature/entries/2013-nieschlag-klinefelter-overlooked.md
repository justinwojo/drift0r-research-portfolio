# Summary

- Klinefelter syndrome (KS; typically 47,XXY) is among the most common congenital chromosomal disorders in males (≈0.1–0.2% of newborn males) yet is heavily underdiagnosed—only about one-quarter of affected individuals receive a lifetime diagnosis.
- Adult hallmarks: small firm testes, androgen-deficiency signs (sparse male-pattern body/facial hair, reduced libido, erectile dysfunction), infertility with azoospermia in >90%, and frequent comorbidities including gynecomastia, osteoporosis, metabolic syndrome/type 2 diabetes.
- Diagnosis is chromosomal (karyotype); clinical suspicion should trigger testing rather than relying on a “classic textbook” full phenotype.
- Testosterone therapy is the mainstay when T falls below treatment thresholds, alongside comorbidity surveillance and fertility counseling (occasional TESE/ICSI success).

# Key data points

- Incidence estimates ≈1:500–1:1000 males; diagnostic yield remains low relative to prevalence.
- Core physical clue often missed: small, firm testes; laboratory pattern is **primary** (hypergonadotropic) hypogonadism—low/low-normal T with elevated LH/FSH.
- Comorbid list explicitly includes osteoporosis, thrombosis risk, varicose veins, epilepsy, learning/language issues in childhood.
- Treatment threshold discussed: initiate T if concentration drops below ≈12 nmol/L, following hypogonadism guidelines (no large RCTs specific to young KS).

Confirmed thiamine-responsive beriberi, HαT, IgG4 skew, and hypercalciuria are outside the KS core.
- **Why catalogued:** Defines the underdiagnosis problem and the phenotype checklist against which karyotype is rationally ordered or deferred.

# Caveats

- Review, not a prospective cohort; phenotype severity is skewed toward diagnosed (often more classic) cases.
- Does not replace LH/FSH, testicular exam, and semen analysis before labeling “rule out KS.”
- Mosaic KS (≈10%) can be milder and harder to recognize clinically.
