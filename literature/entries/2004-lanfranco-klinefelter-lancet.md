# Summary

- KS is the most common genetic cause of male infertility, yet many cases remain undiagnosed because of substantial clinical variability and insufficient clinician awareness.
- Extra X chromosome(s) drive progressive testicular failure after puberty: small firm testes, hypergonadotropic hypogonadism, azoospermia/severe oligozoospermia.
- Phenotype is a spectrum—from near-classic (tall stature, gynecomastia, sparse body hair, eunuchoid proportions) to men identified only at infertility evaluation.
- Early recognition matters for timely androgen replacement, bone and metabolic surveillance, psychosocial support, and fertility planning.

# Key data points

- Karyotype 47,XXY is the majority form; mosaics (e.g., 46,XY/47,XXY) and higher-grade aneuploidies exist and modify severity.
- Laboratory signature of classic KS: elevated FSH (and usually LH) with low or low-normal testosterone—not isolated low T with low/normal gonadotropins.
- Androgen deficiency contributes to reduced muscle mass, body-hair sparsity, gynecomastia risk, and low bone mass.
- Diagnosis requires cytogenetic confirmation; clinical suspicion + primary hypogonadism should prompt karyotype.
