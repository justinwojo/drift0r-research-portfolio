# Summary

- Review of the relationship between dietary salt (sodium chloride) intake and kidney stone risk.
- High sodium intake increases urinary calcium excretion (and can lower citrate), raising calcium-stone risk even when serum calcium is normal.
- Mechanism centers on volume-expansion effects that reduce proximal tubular sodium and calcium reabsorption (coupled transport).
- Supports sodium restriction as a first-line, low-toxicity lever to lower calciuria in hypercalciuric stone formers—aligned with urology advice already given in this case.

# Key data points

- Higher salt intake associated with hypercalciuria and hypocitraturia—major calcium-stone risk factors.
- Classic physiologic data: large step-ups in dietary Na produce large rises in calciuria (historical loading studies show tens of percent increases across extreme Na ranges).
- Approximate population estimate often cited in related literature: ~20–40 mg extra urine Ca per ~100 mmol (≈2.3 g) incremental dietary sodium (also summarized in Ryan & Ing 2018).
- Dietary Na reduction is synergistic with thiazides and fluid advice; high urine Na can blunt thiazide anticalciuric effect.
- Distinguishes dietary sodium from “salt” labeling confusions and measurement issues (24h urine Na as intake proxy).

# Caveats

- Sodium restriction helps calciuria but will not by itself reverse markedly low BMD.
- 24h urine Na reflects recent intake; needs repeated collections for titration.
- Over-restriction risks in other contexts (e.g., volume depletion) should be individualized.
