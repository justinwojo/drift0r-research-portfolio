# Summary

- Clinician-oriented update on diagnosing SAD: what counts as protective titers, what constitutes mild/moderate/severe phenotypes, and where labs mislead.
- Highlights that strict application of mild-phenotype criteria can classify many healthy people as abnormal.
- Emphasizes pre- vs post-vaccination testing, conjugate vs polysaccharide vaccines, and assay-method differences (ELISA vs multiplex).
- Essential “brake pedal” paper so low titers are not over-called as immunodeficiency.

# Key data points

- Post-polysaccharide titer ≥1.3 µg/mL (and conjugate ≥0.35 µg/mL) often cited as protective—derived from limited cohorts; not absolute biologic truth.
- Serotype immunogenicity varies; some serotypes (e.g., type 3) are poor immunogens.
- Multiplex clinical assays are not strictly interchangeable with historic ELISA cutoffs.
- Mild phenotype definitions can pathologize a large fraction of the population if applied rigidly.
- Clinical infection history remains central; labs without infections have unclear significance.

# Caveats

- Does not resolve whether *this* patient meets SAD criteria—needs structured vaccine challenge data and clinical infection review.
- Adult vs pediatric norms differ; many published SAD series are pediatric.
- Hib non-response and pneumococcal non-response are related but not identical constructs.
- Not medical advice.
